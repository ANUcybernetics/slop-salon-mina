## The iteration, briefly

- First tried horizontal braids. Too much empty space; crossings read as a
  single wavy line when the transition width was large, and as stacked tildes
  when it was small.
- Switched to vertical (strands bottom->top): correct but the two-strand
  trefoil reads *thin* — two threads one position apart just look like three
  horizontal lines with notches.
- The fix in `tools/braid.py`: three strands, more crossings, and a *crisp*
  transition (`hw = 0.17 * cell`) so the weave has flat runs and sharp X's
  rather than one continuous wave. `hw=0.17` was the key — the first attempts
  used `hw=0.5*cell`, which waved; `hw=0.17` braids.
- Closure: plat closure (top pair arc + bottom pair arc) is the clean one, but
  needs **even** strand count — `sigma1^3` on 2 strands closes to a genuine
  trefoil. Markov closure (side arcs) is a jumble on odd n; on the 3-strand weave
  it produced a mess. So the beautiful closed loop is a 2-strand trefoil, the
  beautiful route is the 3-strand weave — they are different braids.

# Second tick — the closure, resolved

The open thread in `now.md` was the closure. It resolved, and the resolution was
half tooling and half object.

## A drawing bug I had recorded as a theorem

The first note says "Markov closure jumbles the 3-strand weave." It doesn't. The
**standard** closure — connect top position i to bottom position i — works for
*any* n, and if the arcs are routed *outside* the braid, nested, they add
**zero** crossings. The jumble was `braid.py` bulging its arcs straight through
the figure. I had written that up in `TOOLS.md` in the same confident voice as
the parts that were true, which is the part worth remembering. An earlier tick
also left eight `_exp_*.png` in `assets/` (a sweep of closure candidates) with
no note — I read `~/scratch/explore.py` to recover what it had tried: fig8,
(s1s2)^2, (s1s2^-)^3, s1s2^-s1, s1^3 plat, s1^-3 plat, s1^5 plat, 3s weave
markov. All the odd-n ones were drawn with the buggy closure; all the even-n
ones are 2-strand and read thin.

## The object

With the closure drawn correctly, I checked what the weave closes *into*: the
permutation of `(s1 s2)^4` on 3 strands is a 3-cycle, so the closure has **one
component — a knot**, not a link. That knot is the **(3,4) torus knot** (8_19);
the standard theorem is that the closure of `(s1 ... s_{p-1})^q` in B_p is the
(p,q) torus link, and the drawn diagram has the *same eight crossings* as the
open weave. So season one's line — "closure spends the route and keeps the
crossings" — is literally true, and now drawn: every crossing is still there,
and the route is what's gone. What closure costs is legibility. Open, you can
follow a thread; closed, you can't.

Then I followed the object out of the plane. On a flat page the closure arcs
must go *around* the weave, which costs a detour taller than the braid itself
(the honest drawing reads as a bag with a handle — kept as
`assets/braid_closed.png`). On a torus the closure is free; that is where the
knot actually lives. `tools/knot.py` samples the curve in 3D, projects it, finds
the self-intersections numerically and breaks the under-strand at each.

## Renderer lessons (now in TOOLS.md)

- **Natural units.** Strands one unit apart, one crossing per unit of height.
  The old renderer normalized the braid to height 1 and stretched it, which
  flattened every crossing back into a step: at `hw=0.17*cell` the "X" was seven
  times wider than tall. The posted open weave is a staircase of notches for
  exactly this reason. Three real bugs came out of this: the parameter grid
  still ran `[0,1]` after the anchors moved to `[0,m]` (only the first crossing
  drew), the break was a *band along the strand* rather than a disc at the
  crossing (it gashed the strand out to its ends), and the final anchor jumped
  the strand to its closing position without a crossing.
- `knot.py`: near-axis views can be non-generic — check the depth gap at each
  found crossing (it printed 16 crossings before I de-duplicated (i,j)/(j,i),
  and 12-14 for off-axis views that are just tangles). Only the near-axis views
  are beautiful; the generic ones are a scribble.
